Muslims often claim that they respect Jesus and that Islam teaches about himself But what does Islam actually teach about Jesus? How much information does it provide? And is that information accurate?

Absolutely. One important methodological point first: the Qur’an’s statements about Jesus are not all of the same kind. Some are historically or theologically compatible with the Bible, some appear to reflect later Christian apocryphal traditions, some are ambiguous, and some directly contradict the New Testament.

I will therefore distinguish:

* 🟢 Biblically accurate/compatible — the Qur’an’s statement corresponds substantially to the canonical Bible.
* 🟡 Partially accurate / compatible with qualification — there is a biblical basis, but the Qur’an changes or oversimplifies the biblical picture.
* 🟠 Apocryphal/non-canonical tradition — the claim is not found in the Bible but has a recognizable parallel in later Christian writings or traditions.
* 🔴 Contradicts the Bible — the Qur’anic claim conflicts with the canonical New Testament.
* ⚪ Not independently verifiable from the Bible — the Qur’an makes a claim for which the Bible neither confirms nor directly denies it.

# The most important comparison

When the Qur’anic material is taken as a whole, an interesting pattern emerges.

The Qur’an preserves a surprisingly large number of historically recognizable features of the New Testament portrait of Jesus:

1. Jesus was born of Mary.
2. Mary was a virgin.

The crucifixion is the decisive dividing line


The Qur’an says:

“They killed him not, nor crucified him” (Qur’an 4:157).

The New Testament, by contrast, makes the crucifixion absolutely central:

* Jesus predicted his death: Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34.
* The Last Supper interprets his death: Matthew 26:28.
* The crucifixion is recorded by all four Gospels: Matthew 27; Mark 15; Luke 23; John 19.
* Paul received it as part of the earliest Christian tradition: 1 Corinthians 15:3–4.
* Peter publicly preached it: Acts 2:23–24.
* The resurrection is meaningless in the apostolic preaching without the preceding death: 1 Corinthians 15:14–17.

Indeed, Paul states:

“Christ died for our sins … he was buried … he was raised on the third day” (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).

So from a Christian standpoint, this is not a peripheral disagreement. The Qur’an and the apostolic Gospel give fundamentally incompatible accounts of what happened to Jesus at the climax of his earthly ministry.

# Apocrypal sources

The clay-bird story in Qur’an 3:49 and 5:110 is particularly interesting because it has a close parallel in the Infancy Gospel of Thomas, an apocryphal Christian writing that predates Islam.

In that story, the child Jesus fashions birds out of clay and gives them life.

That story is not in Matthew, Mark, Luke, or John.

This makes it an excellent example of why we need to distinguish:

Qur’anic Jesus ≠ simply the biblical Jesus.

The Qur’an appears to preserve a mixture of:

* genuine Gospel traditions,
* Jewish-Christian themes,
* theological reinterpretations,
* and traditions circulating outside the canonical New Testament.

The same observation applies to Jesus speaking as an infant. The canonical Gospels never describe this, whereas later infancy literature does.

Funny how the Quran claims that stories are true that we know for a historical fact were made up centuries after the time of Jesus. 

# The Trinity

A particularly important correction concerning the Trinity.

The historic Christian doctrine is:

One God → three persons → Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

It is not "three gods", nor is it God + Jesus + Mary.

The Nicene Christian confession is that the Father, Son, and Spirit possess the one divine nature.

Consequently, Qur’an 5:116 is especially problematic from a historical perspective because it asks Jesus whether he told people to take him and his mother as gods besides Allah. That is not Nicene Trinitarianism.

The biblical Trinity is established instead through passages such as:

* Matthew 28:19
* John 1:1–18
* John 14–16
* 2 Corinthians 13:14
* Hebrews 1:1–14
* Philippians 2:5–11

And the New Testament’s most striking point is that Jesus receives the divine worship that belongs to the one God of Israel.

For example:

> “My Lord and my God!” — John 20:28.

Thomas is not corrected or rebuked for calling Jesus God.

Likewise, Philippians 2:10–11 applies to Jesus language from Isaiah 45:23, where Yahweh declares that every knee will bow to him.

In short, the Quran completely gets the trinity doctrine wrong. It is not about three gods. It is definitely not about God, Jesus and Mary. If the Quran criticized the trinity doctrine that is fair enough, but the least thing you could expect is that Allah - the all-knowing - would at least know what the doctrine actually says. But apparently Allah knows less than a Christian catechumen.

# Bottom line

From an orthodox Christian perspective, the Qur’an gives us a mixed portrait of Jesus.

It gets a substantial number of historical facts right — particularly his miraculous birth, Jewish context, ministry to Israel, miracles, disciples, righteousness, and extraordinary status.

It also preserves several non-canonical infancy traditions, most notably the clay birds and speaking infant.

But on the central theological and historical claims of Christianity, it departs decisively from the New Testament:

The Qur’an denies the deity of Christ, denies the Sonship of Christ, denies the crucifixion, rejects the atoning significance of Christ’s death, rejects his worship, and substitutes a later prophetic figure for the biblical promise of the Holy Spirit.

And that creates a fundamental apologetic problem for Islam: if the Qur’an’s positive claims about Jesus are accepted because Jesus was a genuine messenger from God, why should we reject the earlier eyewitness testimony of the apostles precisely where it conflicts with the Qur’an?

The Christian answer is that the New Testament is earlier, rooted in the apostolic eyewitness tradition, and makes the crucified and risen Christ the center of its proclamation. The Qur’an comes centuries later and contradicts that central testimony.

The decisive Christian confession therefore remains:

“Jesus Christ is Lord” (Philippians 2:11),

and

“Christ died for our sins … he was buried … he was raised on the third day” (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).

Those claims are not secondary details of Christianity. They are the heart of the gospel.
